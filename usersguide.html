Users Guide for Module Builders

This guide is intended to help individuals who are new to Free-moN get started building modules, prepare for their first show, and explore optional design features that can enhance the modular railroading experience. It is a companion to the Free-moN Standards and Recommendations document, which defines the mandatory requirements every module must meet. When in doubt, consult the standard — this guide explains the why and the how behind those requirements.

1. Getting Started

Free-moN is a modular N scale railroading standard built around the concept of individual ownership. You build your own module (or modules) to a common interface specification, then bring them to shows where they connect with other builders' modules to form a complete layout. Because only the endplate interface is standardized, you have enormous creative freedom in the middle of your module.

Before you pick up a saw or a soldering iron, read the Free-moN Standards and Recommendations in full. The standard is not long, and understanding every requirement before you build will save you significant rework later. The FAQ is also an excellent resource that explains the intent behind many of the standards.

A few key points to internalize early:

  • Rail height at the endplate must be 50 inches from the floor (nominal), with legs that are vertically adjustable ±1 inch to handle uneven floors.
  • Track at the endplate must be Code 55 nickel-silver, perpendicular, straight, and level for at least 4 inches in from the outside face of the endplate.
  • Modules connect without rail joiners — track alignment and clamping do the work, so precision construction at the endplate is critical.
  • Electrical connections use standardized Anderson Powerpole connectors; control uses DCC.

Once you have read the standard, join the Free-moN Facebook group and/or the Free-moN Google Group. Introduce yourself, share your plan, and ask questions — the community is very welcoming to new builders.

2. Building Your Module

2.1 Planning Your Module

Start by deciding what you want to model and how the module will fit into a typical layout configuration. Common beginner choices are a simple straight module (one endplate at each end, track running straight through) or a gentle curve. Both are forgiving to build and easy to integrate at any show.

Sketch your track plan before you buy materials. Think about:

  • Module footprint and shape. Rectangular is simplest, but L-shapes, curves, and wyes are all possible once you understand the standard.
  • Number of endplates. A basic module has two. Corner modules have two endplates at a 90° angle; wye modules have three.
  • Single vs. double track. Single track is the most common. Double track modules allow multi-builder passing sidings, but require a transition module with one single-track and one double-track endplate.
  • What you will model. Consider your prototype, era, and geographic region. Think about interesting industries, scenery features, or operational opportunities that will make your module compelling at a show.
  • Transport. How will you move it? Large modules require larger vehicles or trailers. Many builders design modules in sections that break apart for transport and reassemble for a show.

Produce a dimensioned drawing of your module, including the track plan, before you start construction. You will need this to share with show coordinators when registering for an event.

2.2 Framework & Endplates

The endplates are the most critical part of your module. They must be flat, square, and precisely constructed, because they are the interface between your module and everyone else's.

  • Use 3/4 inch birch plywood (or equivalent warp-resistant material) for endplates. Avoid dimensional lumber — it warps.
  • Endplates shall be 6 inches high and a minimum 12 inches wide. Make them wider if your module footprint requires it.
  • Module-to-module clamping happens at the endplate — leave adequate flat, unobstructed clamping surface on either side of the track.

The rest of the frame (sides, cross-members, legs) can be built from whatever materials you prefer, provided the result is rigid and flat. Common choices include 1×4 or 1×3 pine for the frame, with a 1/2 inch plywood or foam sub-deck on top.

Legs must support the module free-standing and must be vertically adjustable ±1 inch. Many builders use T-nuts and carriage bolts to achieve this. Rubber tips on the bottom of each leg protect the floor. The nominal rail-head height is 50 inches above the floor; measure from your sub-deck surface and work backwards to determine leg length.

Build everything flat, square, and true. A module that racks or twists will be difficult to align at a show and may cause derailments at the endplate joints.

2.3 Track

The mainline must use Code 55 nickel-silver flex track or equivalent hand-laid track with prototypical tie dimensions and spacing. Code 55 gives a more realistic rail profile than the Code 80 track common in beginner sets, and is one of the key elements of Free-moN's "raising the bar" philosophy.

Critical mainline requirements:

  • Minimum radius: 22 inches.
  • Minimum of 6 inches of straight track between reverse curves.
  • Turnouts must be at least #6.
  • Maximum grade: 2.0% (1/4 inch rise per 12.5 inches of run).
  • Roadbed: 1/8 inch cork or equivalent.

At the endplate, the track must be perfectly perpendicular to the endplate face, straight, and level for at least 4 inches inboard. Rail and track shall be cut flush with the outside face of the endplate. Track for single-track modules is recommended to cross at the center of the endplate; for double-track modules, each rail center is 9/16 inch (0.5625 inch) from the endplate center, giving a track center-to-center spacing of 1.125 inches (1 1/8 inches).

Take extra care with the last 6 inches of track approaching each endplate. Even a slight kink or twist in this area will cause problems when trains cross between modules at a show. Use a straightedge to verify alignment before ballasting.

Turnout frogs, points, and point rails must be powered in a manner that does not rely solely on wheel-to-point contact. This is typically done with a stationary decoder, a Frog Juicer, or by wiring the frog directly to the appropriate rail through a DPDT switch or contacts on the turnout machine.

2.4 Wiring & Electrical

Free-moN uses DCC for layout control. Each module must carry two buses from endplate to endplate: the Track Bus and the Accessory Bus.

Track Bus

  • Use 12 AWG or heavier wire.
  • Terminate at the endplate with 30A Anderson Powerpole connectors stacked vertically (hood up, tongue down), near the center of the endplate. Red connectors are recommended to distinguish the track bus from the accessory bus.
  • The top connector goes to the left rail (as you face the endplate); the bottom connector goes to the right rail.
  • Track feeder wires (from rail to bus) should be 24 AWG or heavier.

Accessory Bus

  • Use 12 AWG or heavier wire.
  • Terminate with 30A Anderson Powerpole connectors stacked horizontally (tongue-to-tongue, hood-to-hood) near the center of the endplate. Black connectors are recommended.
  • This bus powers turnout motors, structure lighting, signals, animation, and other accessories. It normally carries a DCC signal from a separate booster, or may carry 16V AC as a lower-cost alternative.

Wire everything neatly and secure all wiring so it cannot snag on adjacent modules during setup or teardown. Label your Powerpole connectors if in doubt.

2.5 Scenery

This is where your module comes alive. Free-moN encourages high-fidelity prototypical modeling, so take your time and build scenery you are proud of.

Two scenery standards apply at the endplate:

  • The mainline shall be ballasted with a fine, light gray material.
  • The scenery for the first 6 inches at each endplate shall have a flat profile roughly 1/4 inch below the top of the main line rail. This ensures a smooth visual transition to adjacent modules regardless of what they model.

Recommended practices at the endplate:

  • Use generic grass, earth, or sandy terrain — not roads, rivers, buildings, or other features that would look odd abruptly cut off at the module edge.
  • Avoid tall structures or scenery elements within arm's reach of the endplate that could snag sleeves or be accidentally damaged during setup.

Beyond the 6-inch endplate zone, anything goes — detailed industries, mountains, rivers, bridges, tunnels, cities. Study prototype photos and real-world geography for inspiration. The best Free-moN modules tell a story.

2.6 Fascia, Skirting & Crowd Barriers

Fascia covers the visible sides of the module frame between the sub-deck and the top of the legs. It must fully cover each side of the module, be contoured to match the scenic topography, and be painted semi-gloss or satin black. The black fascia creates a visual "frame" for the scenery and ties adjacent modules together visually at a show.

Skirting hangs below the fascia to cover the legs and underside of the module. It must also be black and extend past the endplate on each end to overlap with adjacent module skirting. The bottom edge should be even with the bottom of the leg vertical member so it does not drag on the floor.

Crowd control barriers are recommended at the rate of one stand per side for every 8 feet of module length. Stands consist of a 12-inch square plywood base with a 1/2 inch white PVC pipe upright (36 inches tall) topped with a PVC T-junction through which 1/4 inch yellow nylon rope is threaded. Leave the uprights white — do not paint them.

2.7 Testing Before Your First Show

Before attending a show, thoroughly test your module:

  • Run trains. Run a variety of locomotives and rolling stock — including long passenger cars and articulated locomotives — through every track on your module at various speeds. Look for derailments, hesitations, or electrical dead spots.
  • Check the endplates. Verify that the track is perfectly perpendicular and level at each endplate. Use a straightedge and a small level.
  • Verify your electrical connections. Confirm that your Powerpole connectors are wired correctly (polarity matters!). Borrow or purchase a simple DCC tester or use a multimeter.
  • Test on a flat surface. Place your module on a known-flat surface and check that legs are adjusted to bring the rail head to 50 inches. Verify all four (or more) leg tips contact the surface.
  • Try a mock setup. If you have a friend who also builds Free-moN, do a practice connection to confirm everything aligns and trains cross cleanly. If you don't have a local contact, post in the Facebook group or Google Group — someone nearby may be willing to do a pre-show shakedown.

3. Going to a Show

3.1 Before the Show

Watch the Free-moN Facebook group and Google Group for show announcements. When you find an event you would like to attend, contact the show coordinator to express your intent to participate.

You will need to provide the coordinator with an accurate description of your module so they can plan the layout. Include:

  • Overall dimensions and shape (a dimensioned top-view drawing is ideal).
  • Track plan, including track centers, radii of any curves, and any angles between endplates.
  • Number and type (single/double track) of endplates.
  • Any special operational features (turnouts, grade changes, etc.).

Coordinators use a range of tools — track planning software, CAD, paper templates — so the most important thing is accuracy, not a specific file format.

Before the show, also:

  • Review the Show FAQ.
  • Confirm your locomotive addresses. If two operators arrive with the same DCC address, one will need to reprogram their locomotive. Ask the show coordinator whether a prefix scheme or pre-assigned addresses will be used.
  • Pack clamps (for connecting to adjacent modules) and your Powerpole connecting leads.
  • Bring a track cleaning block or cloth — you will need to clean your track at the show.
  • Confirm your expected arrival time with the coordinator.

3.2 Setup Day

Arrive on time. A layout setup is a coordinated team effort and late arrivals disrupt the plan.

  1. Find the setup/run coordinator upon arrival and identify yourself.
  2. There may be a brief coordination meeting — attend it.
  3. Unload your module carefully. Offer to help others unload once your module is safe. Always follow the owner's instructions when handling someone else's module.
  4. Set your module on its legs in a staging area. Put your clamps and Powerpole leads within easy reach for when your module is connected.
  5. Setup proceeds outward from the "keystone" module — typically a centrally located corner, wye, or multi-end module that anchors the layout orientation. Follow the coordinator's direction for where you slot in.
  6. As each module is joined, check that rail height is consistent (approximately 50 inches, and more importantly that it is level across the joint — floors can be very uneven). Align side-to-side track alignment, then clamp. Ensure clamps grip the endplate and do not pinch any wiring.
  7. Once clamped, the DCC crew will connect the track bus and accessory bus.
  8. Clean the track on your module. Do not use oily or filmy cleaners (such as Wahl's clipper oil, No-Oxide, transmission fluid, or graphite) — these migrate to adjacent modules and cause problems. A dry track eraser or a clean cloth is preferred.
  9. Place any buildings, accessories, or add-ons on your module.
  10. Attach your skirting.
  11. After all modules are connected, a test engine will run the entire layout to confirm reliable operation. This is usually followed by a walkaround briefing where each module owner explains anything operators need to know about their module.

3.3 Running Trains

Once the layout is declared open, grab your throttle, locomotive, and rolling stock and enjoy yourself. A few guidelines to keep things running smoothly for everyone:

  • Stay with your train. Walk with your locomotive, staying near the head end and looking ahead for other traffic.
  • Don't tailgate. Maintain a safe following distance behind other trains.
  • Communicate. Coordinate meets and passes with other operators in advance. At public shows, if you stop to talk to a visitor, park your train in a siding first.
  • Reset switches. If you use a siding or spur, return all turnouts to the mainline position when you are done.
  • Be careful reaching in. Loose sleeves are a common cause of scenery damage. Roll up your sleeves before reaching across a module to couple or uncouple cars.
  • Clean track daily. Module owners should clean their own track at the start of each operating day.
  • If you leave, park your train. Remove it entirely, or ask the run coordinator where you can leave it on a siding. Turn off any sound-equipped locomotives.

3.4 Teardown

The DCC chief will give advance notice before power is cut — use that time to bring your train to a stop and remove it from the layout. Teardown proceeds in the reverse order of setup:

  1. Remove all loose items (trains, accessories, buildings) from your module.
  2. Remove skirting.
  3. Disconnect all electrical connections (Powerpole). Help pick up floor cables from the aisles.
  4. Once all electrical is disconnected, unclamping begins. Do not slide corner or angled modules sideways — this can "unzipper" track from adjacent modules. Wait for unclamping to come to your module in the natural sequence.
  5. Remove legs and repack your module for transport.
  6. Help other builders load until every module and every trailer is packed up. "Many hands make light work."
  7. If required by the venue, help sweep or vacuum the floor.
  8. Celebrate a successful show — there is often an impromptu dinner or gathering afterward!

4. Optional Design Features

The Free-moN standard intentionally specifies only the endplate interface and mainline track. Everything else is up to you. The following optional features are commonly implemented by Free-moN builders and can significantly enhance the visual and operational quality of your module and the layouts it participates in.

4.1 Modular Signal System (MSS)

The Modular Signal System (MSS) is an independent, self-contained signaling system designed specifically for modular railroads. It was developed to allow module builders to add prototypical block signaling to their modules without requiring any central configuration or coordination with other builders. MSS signals operate automatically based on train occupancy and the state of adjacent MSS-equipped modules.

How MSS Works

MSS uses a dedicated 4-wire bus that runs between modules, separate from the DCC track bus and accessory bus. Each MSS-equipped module detects whether a train is present on its block (using current detection, optical sensors, or other means) and communicates its occupancy state to adjacent modules over the MSS bus. Signals on the module then display the correct aspect (red/stop, yellow/approach, green/clear) based on occupancy in the current and next block(s) ahead, implementing a basic 2- or 3-aspect block signal system.

Because MSS is self-configuring, it works correctly even when MSS-equipped modules are mixed with non-MSS modules. A module without MSS simply does not contribute occupancy data, and neighboring MSS modules degrade gracefully.

Why Add MSS?

  • MSS signals add significant prototypical realism to a Free-moN layout with no centralized programming or DCC configuration required.
  • Signals give operators meaningful visual information about track occupancy ahead, improving operating sessions at larger shows.
  • MSS integrates cleanly with Free-moN's modular philosophy — your module works with or without neighboring MSS modules.

Implementing MSS on Your Module

To add MSS to your module:

  1. Install occupancy detection on your mainline block(s). Current detection (a BD20 or equivalent detection module) is the most common approach; it requires resistor-equipped wheelsets on all rolling stock (which is good practice anyway for DCC operations).
  2. Install the MSS logic board for your module. Several open-source and commercial implementations are available. The MSS logic board reads occupancy and drives signal outputs.
  3. Install signal heads appropriate for your prototype — dwarf signals, searchlight signals, position light signals, or color light signals depending on your modeled railroad.
  4. Wire the MSS bus through your module: a 4-conductor cable (often RJ11/RJ12 plugs are used) connecting to jacks at each endplate.
  5. At the show, MSS modules simply plug together; the system self-initializes with no additional configuration.

More information about MSS, including schematics, PCB designs, and protocol specifications, is available through the Free-moN community and the MSS project documentation. Search for "Modular Signal System" or "MSS Free-moN" in the Facebook group or Google Group.

4.2 Double Track Endplates

A module may have double-track endplates, with two tracks crossing the endplate at a center-to-center spacing of 1.125 inches (1 1/8 inches). Double track modules enable multi-builder passing sidings — when your double-track module connects between two other builders' single-track modules, you can provide a siding that would be otherwise impossible to create with single-track modules alone.

If you build a double-track module, it is strongly recommended to also build a transition module with one single-track endplate on one end and one double-track endplate on the other. This allows your double-track module to be used at any show, not just those where another double-track module is adjacent.

4.3 Grades

Grades add visual interest and operational challenge to a layout, but they must be carefully implemented to remain compatible with other modules. A few key rules:

  • Maximum grade is 2.0%.
  • On modules with grades, the elevation difference between the high and low endplate must be a multiple of 3/4 inch. This allows adjacent module builders to compensate with leg adjustment.
  • The maximum height of the rail head is 62 inches above the floor. Since nominal height is 50 inches, you have up to 12 inches of total rise available across a run of grade modules.
  • Communicate clearly with show coordinators that your module has a grade, and provide the elevation difference at each endplate.

4.4 Staging & Yards

Staging modules and yard modules are valuable contributions to a Free-moN layout because they provide a place for operators to stage trains before entering the main layout and add operational complexity and interest. Because inter-section interfaces (within a single module) are not subject to Free-moN interface standards, you have tremendous freedom in building a yard as a multi-section module.

Things to keep in mind when building a yard or staging module:

  • The endplate interfaces still must meet the full Free-moN standard.
  • Yards with many operators in one area benefit from extra DCC throttle access points on the module sides.
  • Consider providing dedicated wiring runs for each yard track to allow independent isolation if needed during operations.
  • Clearly communicate to the show coordinator how many yards tracks are available and which tracks route to the mainline.

4.5 Module Lighting

Structure lighting, interior lighting, street lamps, and other illuminated details dramatically improve the visual impact of a module, especially at indoor public shows where venue lighting may be dim. Lighting can be powered from the Accessory Bus (using a bridge rectifier and voltage regulator as needed) or from a dedicated power supply brought to the show.

LED lighting is highly recommended over incandescent: lower power consumption, longer life, and no heat buildup inside structures. Warm white LEDs (2700–3000K) give a convincing incandescent appearance; cool white or daylight LEDs (5000–6500K) work well for fluorescent-lit industrial buildings.

If you use a DCC stationary decoder to control structure lighting (enabling lighting effects such as flicker, random on/off, or operating schedule), it can be connected directly to the Accessory Bus DCC signal without any additional power supply.

5. Joining the Community

Free-moN thrives on its community of individual builders who share a passion for high-quality N scale modeling. There are no clubs, no dues, and no required memberships — just a shared standard and a shared love of the hobby.

Ways to connect:

  • Free-moN Facebook Group — the most active day-to-day community for show announcements, build photos, and questions.
  • Free-moN Google Group — a longer-running forum with extensive archived discussions and technical detail.
  • Free-moN GitHub Issues — where the Free-moN Steering Committee tracks open questions and potential standard updates. If you have a suggestion or a question about the standard, this is the place to raise it formally.

The Free-moN Steering Committee is a five-member body empowered to update the standard. Changes are made carefully, with community input, and are not taken lightly. By choosing to build to the Free-moN standard you agree to update your modules as the standard evolves. You always have the option to continue operating your existing modules to an older version of the standard at shows that accept them, or to choose a different standard altogether — but the spirit of Free-moN is moving forward together.

Welcome to Free-moN — we look forward to seeing your module on the layout!
